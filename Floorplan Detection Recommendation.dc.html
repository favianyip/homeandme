HomeandMe · floor-plan detection Internal recommendation · draft for review

Why the detector keeps failing, and the three changes that fix it

Recommendation memo 1 August 2026 Floor plan → walkable 3D

The recommendation in four lines

  1. Stop treating this as a detection problem. You are missing a geometry regularisation stage — the step that turns noisy evidence into a watertight wall graph. Walkability comes from there, not from better pixels.
  2. Ship on archetype recognition, not reconstruction. HDB is a closed catalogue of ~20 layouts. Identify and fit a hand-authored model instead of rebuilding one from scratch.
  3. Add a hard validation gate and a 30-second repair editor. Every product in this category has a correction step; zero-touch automation is what is making the worst sheet define the product.
  4. Then, and only then, invest in learned detection — with vector-first ingestion (parse the PDF's real geometry) ahead of any pixel work.

1. What is actually going wrong

The symptom you reported is diagnostic on its own: coverage improved on all fourteen corpus sheets, yet only seven came out fully green, and each per-style fix quietly costs you a different style. That is not the signature of an under-tuned system. It is the signature of a system whose structure cannot express the thing being asked of it. Four causes, in order of how much they cost you.

A local method is being asked to guarantee a global property

"Walkable" is not a pixel property. It means every room is a closed face, every wall meets its neighbours at a resolved junction, and the door graph connects the entrance to every room. Those are facts about the whole plan. Thresholding, morphology and contour tracing are local operations on neighbourhoods of a few pixels. No amount of gate tuning promotes a local operation into a global guarantee — so a mask that is 95% correct still yields a model that leaks, because the 5% is exactly where two wall ends failed to meet.

One parameter set is serving many drawing conventions

HDB sheets span several drafting eras — heavy v1 scans, SA sheets, Simplified, Point Block — with different line weights, hatch conventions, label density and scan noise. Your thresholds must satisfy all of them simultaneously, so improving a Simplified sheet regresses an SA sheet. Drawing style belongs in the pipeline as a branch — classify it first, then apply style-specific parameters or a learned model — never as an average.

The hard 10% is on the critical path

Commercial floor-plan-to-model products keep either a human QA pass or a vector editor in the loop; none claim unattended perfection. By committing to fully automatic output you have made the worst sheet in the corpus the definition of success. Even state-of-the-art research systems on this exact task are wrong on roughly one line in fourteen (see §7) — the correction path is not a failure of ambition, it is the shape of the problem.

Exact geometry is being thrown away before detection starts

Many HDB plans circulate as PDFs that still contain vector content — the walls are already exact line segments with exact coordinates. Rasterising such a file and then re-detecting those lines with computer vision destroys information you were handed for free, and it is the single cheapest accuracy win available to you.

2. The reframe: four stages, one contract

Split the work into stages with different tolerance for error, and let a strict data contract sit between them. The critical insight is that only one stage is allowed to be uncertain.

Stage 0 · Route
Identify the file type and the drawing style. Vector PDF, clean raster, or heavy scan; which HDB era. Choose a lane (§6).
Stage 1 · Understand
Probabilistic — allowed to be wrong. Decide what each primitive or pixel is: structural wall, partition, door, window, fixture, annotation. Outputs evidence with confidences, never geometry.
Stage 2 · Regularise
Deterministic — cannot be wrong. Fit a clean, axis-aligned wall graph to that evidence and enforce closure, snapping and minimum sizes. Watertight by construction, or it fails loudly with named issues.
Stage 3 · Build
Extrude walls, boolean the openings, generate collision and navmesh, export. Trivially correct whenever Stage 2 held.
Stage 4 · Repair
A small editor for the residue: drag a wall, split a room, place a door. Confidence-driven, so the user is pointed at the two things that need attention rather than auditing the whole plan.
You currently have no Stage 2. Tracing is not fitting. The pipeline goes from mask straight to extrusion, which is why accuracy improvements do not convert into walkable models — and why the failures feel unfixable rather than merely wrong.

3. The contract — build this file first

One schema, produced by Stage 2 and consumed by everything downstream: the 2D preview, the 3D walkthrough, the SketchUp and DXF exports, the area schedule. It decouples four workstreams, lets you swap detectors without touching the 3D, and makes "is this plan good?" a question you can answer mechanically.

Figure 1 — The plan contract. Walls are centreline + thickness, never outline polygons. Rooms reference wall ids rather than owning their own coordinates, so a room cannot disagree with the wall it sits against.

4. Stage 2 in detail — the missing algorithm

This is a week or two of work and it is where most of your accuracy is hiding. Every step is deterministic and testable in isolation.

  1. Deskew. Estimate the dominant line orientation across the sheet and rotate the whole plan onto the axes. Keep the residual angle so genuinely diagonal walls survive.
  2. Centrelines, not contours. Pair the two drawn faces of each wall, or take the medial axis of the wall mask. From here on a wall is a segment plus a thickness — outlines are never carried forward.
  3. Cluster the axes. Collect the x-coordinates of all vertical centrelines and cluster them in one dimension with a tolerance of about half a wall thickness; replace each with its cluster mean. Repeat for y. Two hundred nearly-aligned lines collapse onto twenty exact axes, which is what makes everything after this step stable.
  4. Extend, intersect, clean. Extend each segment to its nearest crossing axis within a snap radius, build a planar graph, dissolve collinear degree-two nodes, and drop dangling stubs under ~300 mm. Wall ends that "nearly met" are now the same vertex.
  5. Extract rooms as faces. Find the minimal cycles of the planar graph; discard the outer face; merge anything under ~1.5 m² into its neighbour. This kills the over-segmentation you see on Simplified sheets, because slivers cannot survive as faces.
  6. Project openings onto walls. Snap each door and window candidate to its host wall segment, clamp the width to HDB norms, and reject anything that does not lie inside a wall. An opening that cannot be hosted becomes an issue, not a hole in space.
  7. Quantise. Snap all coordinates to 25 mm at true scale.
  8. Validate and gate. Per §5. Either the model passes or the user is told precisely what to fix.
The property that matters: after step 5, rooms are cycles of the very same wall segments the 3D extrudes. There is no second copy of the geometry to disagree, so a room cannot leak. That is what "walkable by construction" means.

5. Gates and metrics — measure walkability, not rooms

"Rooms detected" and "coverage" are proxies that can improve while the product gets no better, which is exactly what happened. Replace them.

Gate Rule On failure
Watertight Every room is a closed cycle; no open faces or unresolved junctions Block publish; pin the issue on the plan
Reachability Breadth-first search from the entrance across the door graph reaches every room Block; propose the missing door
Area sanity Sum of room areas within 5% of the archetype or printed total Warn; ask the user to confirm one dimension
Orthogonality 95% of walls within 1° of an axis Warn — deskew probably failed
Openings Each opening lies wholly within its host wall, width 0.6–2.4 m Auto-correct or flag
Scale mm-per-pixel within 15% of the matched archetype's expectation Ask for one known dimension

Table 1 — Validation gates. A blocking gate must be able to stop publication; a warning must be visible in the UI, not only in the harness.

The five numbers to track per release: watertight rate (share of sheets producing a leak-free model — your headline figure), reachability rate, median area error, median wall-centreline error in millimetres, and clicks-to-fix — the median number of manual edits needed to make a plan correct. The last one is the only metric your users feel. Target a median of two, and treat any sheet needing more than eight as a failure regardless of its pixel scores.

Table 2 — All four surviving options write the same contract from §3, so they can be developed in parallel and swapped per upload.

Why archetype fitting is the answer for HDB specifically

Free-form reconstruction — "given any drawing, recover any building" — is a research problem. Recognition plus registration is an engineering problem, and your domain is small enough for it: a finite catalogue of flat types, standard wall thicknesses, standard door widths, dimensions printed on the sheet, and a title-block code you already read. So invert the pipeline. Classify which known flat this is, retrieve a model a human authored once, then deform it — scale, mirror, rotate, adjust bay dimensions from OCR — until it matches the drawing.

The output is walkable every single time, because its topology was authored rather than derived. Detection degrades from "must be perfect" to "must pick the right template and measure a few spans", which is a vastly easier target — and when it is wrong, it is wrong in ways a user can see and fix in one click ("this is the mirrored variant"). Your hdb-archetypes.json is already halfway there; it is currently used as a scale hint when it should be the backbone.

8. The dataset you attached, and the one you still need

The train-01 folder is a split of FloorPlanCAD — around five thousand paired raster and SVG files here, where every drawn line carries a semantic and instance label. That is a genuinely valuable asset, but for Lane A, not for your current pipeline: it trains a classifier that labels vector primitives, which is precisely the Lane A task and a much better-posed problem than pixel segmentation. Three things to know before you build on it:

  • It is Chinese residential and commercial CAD, not HDB. Use it to pre-train and to prove the approach, then fine-tune on your own sheets — the drawing conventions differ enough that it will not transfer cleanly on its own.
  • Walls and parking dominate the label distribution, so class imbalance needs explicit weighting or you will get a wall detector that ignores everything else.
  • The public release is licensed for non-commercial use. It is fine for research and evaluation; check the terms before anything derived from it ships inside HomeandMe.

Worth noting what the published results on that benchmark imply for you: line-level accuracy has climbed from roughly 81 to 93 F1 across four years of dedicated research effort. Even the best system is wrong about one line in fourteen. If you were hoping detection alone would deliver a watertight model, that number settles it — Stage 2 and the repair editor are structural requirements, not admissions of defeat.

What you still need is your own corpus. Fourteen sheets cannot distinguish a real improvement from noise, and it is why the harness keeps telling you contradictory things. Get to roughly 150 HDB sheets, stratified about twenty each across v1 scans, SA, Simplified, Model A, Premium, Point Block, DBSS and maisonette. Label only what the gates need: wall centrelines, openings, room polygons, and the true scale. Keep the current fourteen as a fast smoke test and make the 150 the release gate. This corpus, not any model, is the durable asset — it is the thing that tells you whether you are getting better.

9. Getting it into SketchUp cleanly

Do not hand SketchUp a mesh — imported meshes arrive as unnamed triangle soup that no one can edit. Emit from the wall graph instead:

  • DXF with wall centrelines on one layer and openings on another. Imports natively and stays push-pull-able — the fastest path to a usable SketchUp file.
  • A Ruby importer (a couple of hundred lines) that reads the contract JSON and builds named groups: Wall/structural, Room/Living. Best round-trip and the one a professional user will thank you for.
  • glTF for the in-browser walkthrough only.
  • IFC if and when a BIM partner asks — not before.

10. Sequence

Phase Work Exit criterion
Week 1 Freeze the contract and the gates; re-instrument the harness with the new metrics Today's pipeline reports watertight and reachability rates, however bad
Weeks 2–4 Stage 2 regulariser and the repair editor Every sheet that yields any walls yields a watertight model; clicks-to-fix ≤ 5
Weeks 4–6 Lane B: author the top eight HDB types parametrically; retrieval and registration 90% of uploads walkable with ≤ 2 clicks — this is the shippable milestone
Weeks 6–10 Lane A: vector PDF and DXF ingestion, primitive classification Vector sources bypass CV entirely; area error under 2%
Next quarter Lane C: learned segmentation on your 150-sheet corpus, pre-trained on FloorPlanCAD The scan lane matches Lane B's watertight rate

Table 3 — Note the order: the deterministic and authored work comes first, the machine learning last. That is deliberate — the ML is only worth its cost once a regulariser can absorb its mistakes.

11. What I would do this week

  1. Write the contract schema and a validator, and make SketchMyFlat emit it alongside the 3D model.
  2. Add the two blocking gates — watertight and reachability — and let the tool refuse to publish a leaky model, with the reason named on screen. A visible honest failure is worth more than a silent broken walkthrough.
  3. Re-baseline the fourteen sheets against the new metrics. Expect the numbers to look worse than today's; they will be true.
  4. Pick the eight HDB types that cover most of your uploads and start authoring them by hand. This is unglamorous and it is the highest-leverage work on the list.
  5. Freeze threshold tuning on the live tool. All parameter work moves to the lab harness, where a change can be measured against the whole corpus rather than the sheet in front of you.
Kill criterion. If after the regulariser is in place you still cannot reach a 95% watertight rate on sheets where walls are found, the bottleneck is Stage 1 evidence quality. At that point buy detection — a hosted vectorisation service or a human QA pass — rather than starting a model-training programme. Deciding that in six weeks costs you six weeks; discovering it in six months costs you the product.
