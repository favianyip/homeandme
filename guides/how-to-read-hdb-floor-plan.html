GUIDE 01 · FUNDAMENTALS

How to read an HDB floor plan without guessing

A floor plan shows how rooms, openings and circulation relate to one another. It cannot answer every renovation question by itself. Work from the exact drawing in front of you, record what it actually shows, and keep that evidence separate from your assumptions.

Start with the exact plan and its legend

Before studying the rooms, identify the document you are reviewing. Keep its full page, title or reference information, revision or date if shown, and legend together. Do not crop away notes merely to make the drawing easier to view.

  1. Is this the plan for the flat you intend to discuss?
  2. Is a revision or date stated?
  3. Is a legend present, and can you read it clearly?
  4. Are any notes, dimensions or parts of the page missing?

Treat unanswered questions as ambiguities. A similar-looking plan found elsewhere is not a substitute for your exact document. This matters for both BTO and resale homes: a generic example may help you form questions, but it is not evidence about your flat.

HOW TO USE THIS

This guide offers a review framework, not a universal symbol dictionary. Plan formats and legends differ. If a mark is unclear, use the legend on that exact plan or seek verification rather than assigning a meaning from memory.

Orient the plan before reading details

Begin with the information that is visibly stated. Locate room labels, entrances, openings and the relationships between spaces. If the drawing provides orientation information, use it; do not invent a compass direction when it does not.

Then trace a simple journey through the home: entrance to shared areas, shared areas to private rooms, and rooms to nearby openings. This helps you notice adjacency and circulation questions without pretending to know dimensions or site conditions that are not shown.

Orientation is about understanding the page, not proving what exists on site. A label shows what the drawing calls a space. It does not verify current finishes, concealed services, furniture fit or construction condition.

Read symbols by category, not from memory

Instead of trying to memorise a supposed list of standard HDB symbols, sort visible marks into questions:

  • Boundaries and openings. Which lines, gaps and arcs appear to define spaces or access? What does this plan’s legend call them?
  • Fixtures and service-related marks. Which items are explicitly identified by the legend or notes? Which remain unclear?
  • Labels and annotations. What room names, abbreviations, dimensions, levels or references are actually printed?
  • Graphic conventions. Are line weights, hatching or dashed lines explained on this page?

This category method keeps uncertainty visible. Never use a generic icon or line style to decide that a wall is removable, structural or suitable for alteration, and do not infer concealed services from an unexplained mark. Those questions require the correct source information and qualified verification.

Treat dimensions and scale as evidence with limits

Give priority to dimensions explicitly written on the plan, while still checking that they are legible and attached to the element you think they describe. A printed scale is useful only when the page has retained its intended size and reproduction quality.

A downloaded image, screenshot, photocopy or resized scan may no longer preserve the printed scale. Printer settings, page fitting, cropping and image resizing can all break a ruler-based reading. If the source or reproduction is uncertain, do not convert pixels or ruler measurements into claimed real-world dimensions.

Even a clear stated dimension is plan evidence, not a site measurement. Before ordering fitted items or making irreversible decisions, measure the relevant condition on site and obtain the appropriate qualified checks. Keep units and assumptions visible rather than silently rounding them.

What a floor plan cannot prove

  • the current as-built condition of a resale flat;
  • concealed structure or services;
  • that a wall or element may be removed or altered;
  • regulatory or renovation approval;
  • exact furniture, appliance or built-in fit;
  • site levels, finishes, damage or access constraints not recorded on the drawing;
  • cost, timeline or construction feasibility;
  • that a concept visualisation is construction-ready.

If an answer affects safety, approvals, construction or expensive fitted work, route it to site measurement and the appropriate qualified professional or official process.

BTO and resale plans need different checks

For a BTO flat

Confirm that the plan belongs to the correct flat and keep any displayed source, revision, notes and options with it. Record what is still unknown before physical access. A planning concept made before site access remains provisional; later site checks may change it. Continue with our BTO layout-planning guide.

For a resale flat

Treat the drawing and the existing flat as two evidence sources that may not fully agree. Renovations, wear, inaccessible areas or undocumented conditions may affect what is found on site. Record differences instead of editing the plan to make them disappear. Continue with our resale layout-planning guide.

A review-first checklist

  • Keep the complete plan and identify its source.
  • Record the revision or date if one is shown.
  • Use the legend on that exact plan.
  • List every unclear symbol, note and cropped area.
  • Separate stated dimensions from ruler- or pixel-derived estimates.
  • Mark any resized scan or uncertain reproduction as unsuitable for scale measurement.
  • Check important dimensions and existing conditions on site.
  • Route structural, service, approval and construction questions to qualified verification.
  • Keep human review and corrections visible before comparing design concepts.

A concept can support discussion after this review. It remains a proposal: non-as-built, non-construction, and not proof of feasibility or approval. If a future 3D workflow is introduced, it must keep the evidence stages separate: address context, source floor plan, human-reviewed geometry before any 3D, design choice, then a separately reviewed itemised quote.

FREQUENTLY ASKED

On symbols, scale and what a plan proves

Are the dimensions on an HDB floor plan exact enough to order furniture?

Do not assume so. Read stated dimensions carefully, then verify critical fit on site before ordering or building. A scan or print may have been resized, and the drawing does not prove every current site condition.

Can I measure a scanned plan with a ruler?

Only if you can establish that the reproduction preserved the intended scale and page size. If it was resized, cropped, fitted to a page or screenshotted, ruler measurements are unreliable. Use stated dimensions and site measurement instead.

What should I do when a symbol is unclear?

Check the legend and notes on that exact plan. Record the symbol as unresolved if the explanation is absent or illegible. Do not borrow a meaning from another drawing and present it as fact.

Can a floor plan tell me whether a wall can be removed?

No. That decision requires the correct property information, current official requirements and qualified verification — never an unexplained graphic convention.

Is a floor plan a construction drawing or an as-built survey?

Do not treat it as either merely because it looks detailed. Confirm the document’s stated purpose and obtain the appropriate site and professional evidence for construction decisions.

Why do I still need site measurements?

They check the current physical conditions that matter to fit and execution, and they surface differences between a drawing and a resale flat’s existing condition. Site measurement does not replace any other qualified or approval requirement.
